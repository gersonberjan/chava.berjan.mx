In January 2017, I published an article titled [*La nube: un cambio de paradigma*](https://xecureblog.wordpress.com/2017/01/31/la-nube-un-cambio-de-paradigma/) (The cloud: a paradigm shift).

Today, the range of available services has expanded, and my understanding of the cloud has changed as well.

Back then, I saw a trend that seemed quite clear: many companies would begin moving part of their on-premises infrastructure to the cloud.

And they did.

But the opposite happened, too.

Over the years, we have seen organizations adopt cloud services, retain their own infrastructure, build hybrid environments, and even move certain workloads back from the cloud to on-premises systems.

We also had to learn new ways of managing and optimizing the cost of those services, adopting practices such as [FinOps](https://www.finops.org/introduction/what-is-finops/), and comparing this model with concepts we already knew, such as investment in and depreciation of owned assets.

So, did that paradigm shift really happen?

I think it did. **The change was simply bigger than I had imagined.**

## A phrase that took me years to experience firsthand

Long before that article, I kept hearing a phrase:

> “A company that isn't on the Internet will disappear.”

I understood it and had made it my own.

**But understanding what it meant was very different from experiencing it.**

For a long time, “being on the Internet” could mean having a website, email, a published system or the beginnings of a digital service offering.

In 2020, I truly understood what it could mean.

The pandemic brought restrictions. In Jalisco, and later across much of Mexico and the world, going out and spending time in workplaces carried a risk of infection. Many in-person activities stopped or were restricted, except those considered essential.

Almost overnight, many companies had to answer a question they might not have been prepared for:

**Can we keep operating if people cannot physically be here?**

The Internet was no longer just about having a presence.

**It became the means of operating.**

Working, serving customers, selling, collaborating, accessing information and keeping processes running remotely were no longer optional capabilities. For many organizations, they became essential to business continuity.

That was when I understood the phrase differently.

The change wasn't merely about companies being on the Internet.

It was about their ability to **operate digitally**.

## So, what is the cloud?

After all these years, I see it from two perspectives.

The first is technical: to a large extent, the cloud is **someone else's data center**. Behind it are still servers, storage, networks, power, redundancy, replicas, maintenance, standards and teams of people operating the infrastructure.

The second is as a **platform of capabilities**. We can deploy services quickly, automate, use managed services, work from baselines and access advanced enterprise solutions without having to build all the underlying infrastructure ourselves.

This second perspective also introduces an important concept: **shared responsibility**. Some responsibilities belong to the provider, while others remain ours.

## An opportunity to invest in the business

One thing that especially interested me back then was what the cloud could mean for a new company.

Imagine a newly established organization.

Before generating revenue, it may need servers, storage, communications, backups, security, redundancy and people capable of operating all of it.

Building those capabilities requires capital.

The cloud offers another possibility: consuming many of these capabilities as they are needed.

This can help a company avoid tying up its capital in infrastructure from day one and instead invest more of its resources in what actually generates business value.

That doesn't mean the cloud is automatically cheaper.

Nor does it mean it is the right answer for every workload.

It means we have another way to consume technology and another tool for designing operations.

## So, cloud or on-premises?

This is probably one of the things that has changed most in how I think about it.

If a company already has data centers, depreciated infrastructure, staff, connectivity, procedures and stable workloads, my response wouldn't be:

> “Let's move everything to the cloud.”

It would be:

**Let's analyze your operations.**

Let's look at what you have, what you need, which problems you want to solve, and whether operating something in the cloud makes sense.

Some workloads may be excellent candidates for the cloud.

Others may be better suited to on-premises infrastructure.

And still others may become part of a hybrid architecture.

The objective shouldn't be to say that we're “in the cloud.”

The objective should be for technology to support the business effectively.

## Almost ten years later

In 2017, I saw the cloud as a paradigm shift.

**Almost ten years later, I still believe it was one, but my understanding of that shift has evolved.**

Technology changed over those years, but I also learned to make better decisions about where and how to use it.

And perhaps that is the most important lesson for me.

Today, we're experiencing something similar with artificial intelligence. It seems everything needs AI because it is the technology transforming the industry.

Before that, it was cloud.

Tomorrow, it will be something else.

But the question should remain the same:

**What does the business need?**

Not everything needs to be in the cloud to be done well.

Not everything needs to be on-premises.

And not everything requires AI.

**Let's not adopt capabilities because they're fashionable. Let's adopt them because the business needs them.**
